# Stochastic Differential Equation Simulation

Educational Python project focused on the numerical simulation of stochastic processes used in quantitative finance.

The project mainly studies the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and then introduces the Heston model as an extension.

The objective is to connect:

* the mathematical equations;
* their probabilistic interpretation;
* their Python implementation;
* their validation through simulation.

## Project Objectives

* understand the structure of a stochastic differential equation;
* simulate a process using the Euler–Maruyama scheme;
* study the mean-reverting behavior of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process;
* compare simulated results with theoretical results;
* compare Euler–Maruyama with the exact transition of the OU process;
* estimate the parameters of an OU process from synthetic data;
* introduce stochastic variance with the Heston model;
* generate correlated Brownian shocks;
* use Monte Carlo simulation to price options.

## 1. Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is defined by:

```text
dX(t) = theta × (mu - X(t)) × dt + sigma × dW(t)
```

with:

* `X(t)`: value of the process at time `t`;
* `mu`: long-run mean level;
* `theta`: speed of mean reversion;
* `sigma`: intensity of the random noise;
* `W(t)`: Brownian motion.

The process is attracted toward the level `mu` while continuously being disturbed by random shocks.

### Euler–Maruyama Scheme

To simulate the process, time is divided into small intervals `dt` and the following approximation is used:

```text
X(k+1) = X(k)
         + theta × (mu - X(k)) × dt
         + sigma × sqrt(dt) × Z(k)
```

where:

```text
Z(k) follows a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.
```

The update contains two components:

* a deterministic term that pulls the process back toward `mu`;
* a random term generated by Brownian motion.

---

## 2. Theoretical Properties of the OU Process

The theoretical mean of the process is:

```text
E[X(t)] = mu + (X(0) - mu) × exp(-theta × t)
```

As time becomes large:

```text
E[X(t)] tends toward mu.
```

The theoretical variance is:

```text
Var(X(t))
=
sigma² / (2 × theta)
×
(1 - exp(-2 × theta × t))
```

In the long run:

```text
Var(X(t)) tends toward sigma² / (2 × theta).
```

The process therefore has a stationary normal distribution:

```text
Stationary mean = mu

Stationary variance = sigma² / (2 × theta)
```

The project numerically verifies these results by comparing:

* the empirical mean of simulated paths with the theoretical mean;
* the empirical variance with the theoretical variance;
* the histogram of terminal values with the stationary normal density.

---

## 3. Comparison with the Exact Transition

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, the exact transition between two dates is known:

```text
X(t + dt)
=
mu
+
(X(t) - mu) × exp(-theta × dt)
+
sigma
×
sqrt(
    (1 - exp(-2 × theta × dt))
    /
    (2 × theta)
)
× Z
```

where `Z` follows a standard normal distribution.

The project compares this exact simulation with the Euler–Maruyama scheme.

This comparison shows that:

* Euler–Maruyama introduces discretization error;
* this error becomes more visible when the time step is large;
* the approximation improves as the time step decreases.

## 4. Naive Calibration of the OU Process

The project also contains a simple calibration procedure for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

Synthetic paths are first generated using known parameters. The objective is then to recover these parameters using only the observed values.

The expected transition is approximated by:

```text
X_predicted(k+1)
=
X(k)
+
theta × (mu - X(k)) × dt
```

The corresponding residual is:

```text
error(k)
=
X(k+1)
-
X_predicted(k+1)
```

The parameters `theta` and `mu` are estimated by minimizing the mean squared error:

```text
MSE
=
mean of error(k)²
```

The optimization is performed using:

```python
scipy.optimize.minimize
```

Once `theta` and `mu` have been estimated, the volatility is obtained from the dispersion of the residuals:

```text
estimated sigma
=
sqrt(
    mean of error(k)²
    /
    dt
)
```

### Example Result

| Parameter                    | True Value | Estimated Value |
| ---------------------------- | ---------: | --------------: |
| Mean-reversion speed `theta` |     1.5000 |          1.5022 |
| Long-run mean `mu`           |     1.0000 |          0.9898 |
| Volatility `sigma`           |     0.3000 |          0.3010 |

The calibration therefore correctly recovers the parameters used to generate the synthetic data.

This method is intentionally simple and mainly serves to illustrate the general principle of model calibration.

## 5. Extension: Heston Model

The Heston model replaces the constant volatility of geometric Brownian motion with stochastic variance.

The asset price follows:

```text
dS(t)
=
r × S(t) × dt
+
sqrt(v(t)) × S(t) × dW_S(t)
```

The variance follows:

```text
dv(t)
=
kappa × (theta - v(t)) × dt
+
xi × sqrt(v(t)) × dW_v(t)
```

The main parameters are:

* `S(t)`: asset price;
* `v(t)`: instantaneous variance;
* `theta`: long-run variance level;
* `kappa`: speed of mean reversion;
* `xi`: volatility of variance;
* `rho`: correlation between price and variance shocks;
* `r`: risk-free interest rate.

The instantaneous volatility is:

```text
sqrt(v(t))
```

For example:

```text
v(t) = 0.04
```

corresponds to a volatility of:

```text
sqrt(0.04) = 0.20 = 20%
```

### Correlated Shocks

Starting from two independent standard normal variables `Z1` and `Z2`, we construct:

```text
Z_stock = Z1
```

and:

```text
Z_variance
=
rho × Z1
+
sqrt(1 - rho²) × Z2
```

Both resulting variables follow standard normal distributions, and their correlation is equal to `rho`.

When `rho` is negative, a fall in the asset price is often associated with an increase in variance.

### Positivity of Variance

The numerical scheme may temporarily generate negative variance values.

A truncation at zero is therefore used:

```text
positive variance = max(variance, 0)
```

This method is simple and suitable for an educational project, although more accurate numerical schemes exist.

### Asset Price Update

Over a small time step, the variance is assumed to remain constant.

An exponential update inspired by geometric Brownian motion is then used:

```text
S(k+1)
=
S(k)
×
exp(
    (r - positive_variance(k) / 2) × dt
    +
    sqrt(positive_variance(k) × dt)
    × Z_stock(k)
)
```

This formulation guarantees that the asset price remains strictly positive because an exponential function is always positive.

## 6. Monte Carlo Pricing under Heston

The current price of a European call option is obtained from the discounted average of its future payoffs.

The call payoff at maturity is:

```text
payoff = max(S(T) - K, 0)
```

The theoretical price is:

```text
call price
=
discount factor
×
expected payoff
```

with:

```text
discount factor = exp(-r × T)
```

In Monte Carlo simulation, the expectation is replaced by the average simulated payoff:

```text
estimated price
=
exp(-r × T)
×
mean of max(S_T(i) - K, 0)
```

The standard error of the estimate is calculated as:

```text
standard error
=
standard deviation of discounted payoffs
/
sqrt(number of simulated paths)
```

The project also contains an introduction to implied volatility and the visualization of a volatility skew.

These elements constitute an extension of the main part of the project.

---

## 7. Implied Volatility

Implied volatility is the volatility that, when used in the Black-Scholes model, reproduces a given option price.

The principle is:

```text
Black-Scholes price using implied volatility
=
price obtained under Heston
```

Implied volatility provides a common measure for comparing options with different strikes.

The project computes implied volatility for several strikes in order to visualize a volatility skew.

With a negative correlation between asset price and variance, implied volatilities associated with lower strikes can be higher.

## Project Limitations

This project is educational and is not intended to be a production-ready library.

Its main limitations are:

* Euler–Maruyama introduces discretization error;
* OU calibration is performed on synthetic data;
* the calibration procedure does not provide confidence intervals;
* the Heston simulation uses a simple variance-truncation scheme;
* Monte Carlo prices are subject to statistical estimation error;
* model parameters are not calibrated on real financial-market data;
* dividends and time-varying interest rates are not considered;
* the implementation is not optimized for very large-scale simulations.
